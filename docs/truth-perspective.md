# Truth, Perspective, Meaning, and Stability

**Status:** OPEN QUESTION / HYPOTHESIS

## Author's starting ideas
- A word such as *measurable* may be understood differently by different people.
- A sentence can exist even when the situation it describes is false.
- A proposition must be evaluated according to what it specifically means and asserts; an alternate interpretation does not make the intended assertion true.
- Truth may have features worth studying through relations or stability under change.

## Separate layers
1. **Expression:** a physical or digital inscription, utterance, or other sign.
2. **Interpretation:** rules and context used to understand the expression.
3. **Proposition/content:** what is asserted under that interpretation.
4. **Conditions/world/model:** the circumstances against which the proposition is evaluated.
5. **Truth evaluation:** whether the proposition holds under the chosen semantics and conditions.

The existence of an expression does not entail the truth of its content. If `E` is “this expression exists” and `P` is “the described event occurred,” `E` and `P` are different propositions and can have different truth values.

## Perspective as a candidate relation
A first model could represent interpretation as a function of expression, context, observer, and available information:

`I = f(E, C, O, K)`

where `E` is an expression, `C` the context, `O` an observer or interpretive standpoint, and `K` available knowledge. This is a modeling proposal, not a universal law.

Different interpretations of the same expression do not automatically make all interpretations equally appropriate. We need criteria such as ordinary usage, authorial intent when relevant, explicit definitions, evidence, internal consistency, and stated scope.

## Stability hypothesis
The author proposes investigating whether a claim's stability under changes can characterize something important about truth.

To investigate, define a set of transformations `T` and a validity condition `V(P, c)` for proposition `P` under condition `c`. A stability score, if appropriate, might characterize how often or under which transformations `P` remains valid. But this cannot be equated automatically with truth: a vague claim can be stable, and a conditional truth can cease to apply when its conditions change.

## Questions to resolve
1. Is truth a property of propositions, sentences, models, events, or something else in the framework?
2. What is the intended meaning of “every claim is absolute true, preserved in any way”? Could this refer to the persistence of the expression or proposition rather than the factual truth of its content?
3. Does perspective change the proposition itself, the evidence available, the interpretation, or only belief in the proposition?
4. Which transformations are relevant when evaluating stability?
5. Can stability be measured without confusing it with evidential support or truth?
6. How should ambiguity, indexicals, time-dependent statements, counterfactuals, and fictional propositions be represented?

## Guardrail
Do not collapse truth, stability, robustness, consensus, and evidence into one quantity unless a precise model shows why that is justified.
